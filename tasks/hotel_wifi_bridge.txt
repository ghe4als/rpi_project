what would I need to build to take traveling with me that would allow me to bridge a hotel wifi and my own wifi network, so I only have to configure it while traveling instead of 10 devices for wifi
What you want is a travel router running in WISP/client-router mode. It connects to the hotel’s Wi-Fi as its WAN/internet connection, then creates your own private Wi-Fi network for all your devices.

Conceptually:

text


Hotel Wi‑Fi
    ↓
Travel router in WISP/client mode
    ↓
Your private Wi‑Fi + Ethernet
    ↓
Laptop, phone, tablet, streaming device, etc.
The important distinction is that you want router/NAT mode, not ordinary repeater or bridge mode. WISP mode gives your devices addresses from your own router and keeps them on one consistent private network. Simple repeater mode may leave your devices directly dependent on the hotel’s network and generally won’t provide the isolation and DHCP behavior you want. 
asus.com

You would need:

A compact travel router with:
WISP, “hotel,” or “public Wi-Fi” mode
NAT and DHCP
Its own configurable SSID and password
2.4 GHz and preferably 5 GHz Wi-Fi
Optional Ethernet WAN/LAN port
USB-C power
A short Ethernet cable, in case the hotel provides wired internet
A USB-C power adapter or power bank
Optionally, VPN-client support if you want the router to send all your devices’ traffic through one VPN connection
Travel-router product lines that commonly support this use case include GL.iNet travel routers, TP-Link travel routers with hotspot mode, and ASUS models with WISP/public-Wi-Fi mode. The exact model matters more than the brand. For example, hotspot mode on supported TP-Link routers lets you authenticate to the hotel once and then share the connection through your own private wired or wireless network. 
tp-link.com

At the hotel, the usual procedure is:

Power on the travel router.
Connect your phone or laptop to the router’s temporary setup network.
Open its administration page.
Select WISP, Repeater, Hotspot, or Public Wi-Fi mode—whichever the router calls its NAT-based client mode.
Scan for and select the hotel Wi-Fi.
Enter the hotel Wi-Fi password if required.
Connect your phone or laptop to the router’s private Wi-Fi.
Complete the hotel’s browser-based captive-portal login.
Connect all other devices only to your router’s Wi-Fi or Ethernet.
After that, your devices can retain the same private network name, password, and local IP scheme you use at home. You configure the router for each new hotel, but you don’t reconfigure every device.

A few limitations are worth planning for:

Captive portals: Most can be completed through the router, but some hotels require a MAC-address login or restrict the number of devices. Many routers support MAC cloning to help with this, although it may not work on every hotel network. 
tp-link.com
1
Daily reauthentication: Some public networks expire access periodically, so you may need to log in again. 
asus.com
Device discovery: Because your router is doing NAT, devices on your private network can see one another, but hotel devices generally cannot. Features relying on being on the same network as another guest—such as hotel casting systems—may not work.
Streaming services: Some hotel networks block streaming, gaming, VPNs, or unusual ports regardless of your router.
Wi-Fi performance: If the router uses one radio both to receive hotel Wi-Fi and transmit your private Wi-Fi, performance may be reduced. A dual-radio travel router is preferable.
For a more “home-like” setup, configure the travel router with the same:

Wi-Fi network name
Wi-Fi password
LAN subnet, such as 192.168.50.0/24
DHCP reservations, if you use fixed local addresses
Then your devices should reconnect automatically without knowing whether they are at home or traveling. You may still want a separate travel SSID to avoid accidentally connecting to the wrong network, but technically matching your home settings is possible.

The short answer: buy or build a small router whose Wi-Fi radio acts as a WAN/client connection, while its other radio and Ethernet ports provide your private LAN. Building one from a Raspberry Pi or mini-PC with OpenWrt is possible, but a purpose-built travel router will be smaller, more reliable, and much easier to use at captive portals.

Software options

You have two reasonable approaches.

Easiest: Raspberry Pi OS plus NetworkManager

Use Raspberry Pi OS Lite and configure:

wlan0 as the hotel Wi-Fi client
wlan0 again as your local access point
dnsmasq or NetworkManager for DHCP
iptables or nftables for NAT
This is flexible and gives you a normal Linux system, but simultaneous client/AP operation on one Wi-Fi radio is the awkward part.

More router-like: OpenWrt

OpenWrt is a better conceptual fit because it already provides:

Wireless client configuration
Access-point configuration
DHCP
Firewall
NAT
A web interface through LuCI
However, support and setup on Pi Zero models can be more involved than on purpose-built OpenWrt routers. OpenWrt’s basic wireless setup uses an access-point network, a country code, WPA2/WPA3 security, and a LAN interface with DHCP. 
openwrt.org

Important limitation: one radio, one channel

The Zero W and Zero 2 W use one 2.4 GHz radio. When that same radio connects to the hotel and serves your devices, the hotel connection and your private access point generally need to use the same channel. The Pi may need to change its local AP channel to match the hotel network, briefly disconnecting your devices. There can also be driver and firmware reliability issues with simultaneous client and AP operation. 
Y Combinator

In practice:

It may be perfectly adequate for email, browsing, messaging, and a few devices.
Throughput will be lower than with a real dual-radio travel router.
If the hotel Wi-Fi is on channel 6, your private AP will likely also need to be on channel 6.
If the hotel network changes channels, your private network may briefly drop.
A single client/AP radio cannot provide independent 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz networks.
For a more reliable build, use the Pi’s built-in Wi-Fi only for the hotel connection and add a USB Wi-Fi adapter for your private access point:

text


Hotel Wi‑Fi → Pi Zero W wlan0
                    Pi routing/NAT
Your Wi‑Fi  ← USB Wi‑Fi adapter
That avoids the same-radio channel problem, though you’ll need:

A USB OTG adapter
A compatible USB Wi-Fi adapter
Probably a powered USB hub, depending on power draw
A better alternative is to use USB Ethernet for the private side:

text


Hotel Wi‑Fi → Pi wlan0
                    Pi routing/NAT
             USB Ethernet → separate access point
But that requires carrying a second Wi-Fi access point, so it defeats some of the size advantage.

Basic network layout

Use a private subnet different from the hotel’s likely subnet:

text


Hotel/WAN:        wlan0, DHCP from hotel
Your LAN:         192.168.50.0/24
Pi LAN address:   192.168.50.1
DHCP range:       192.168.50.100–192.168.50.200
Your Pi should:

Obtain an address from the hotel through its Wi-Fi client connection.
Assign itself 192.168.50.1 on the private side.
Run DHCP for your devices.
Enable IPv4 forwarding.
Masquerade/NAT private traffic out through the hotel interface.
Run WPA2-protected Wi-Fi for your devices.
Conceptually, the firewall rule is:

text


private LAN → hotel Wi‑Fi interface → Internet
Do not bridge your private Wi-Fi directly to the hotel Wi-Fi. Use routing/NAT. Bridging makes captive portals, device isolation, and address assignment much less predictable.

Captive portal handling

This is the part that needs deliberate design. When you arrive:

Connect the Pi to the hotel Wi-Fi.
Connect your phone to the Pi’s private SSID.
Open a normal HTTP website to trigger the hotel login page.
Complete the hotel login through the Pi.
Reconnect your other devices to the Pi.
Some hotels identify access by MAC address, restrict connected devices, or require periodic reauthentication. A small setup webpage on the Pi could make this easier, but the first version can simply use SSH or the OpenWrt web interface.

Do I need multiple wifi interfaces, or would it improve performance
You don’t strictly need multiple Wi‑Fi interfaces, but a second interface would improve reliability and usually performance.

With one interface on the Pi Zero W/Zero 2 W, the same radio must:

Receive traffic from the hotel access point.
Transmit traffic to your devices.
Switch back and forth between those jobs.
That creates several limitations:

Your private network generally has to use the hotel’s 2.4 GHz channel.
The radio’s airtime is shared between the hotel connection and your devices.
Practical throughput can be roughly half—or less—of the underlying wireless link speed.
A busy hotel network can make your private Wi-Fi feel sluggish.
Channel changes or hotel access-point roaming may briefly interrupt your network.
The Pi’s built-in radio cannot provide 5 GHz to your devices.
For light use—browsing, email, messaging, and one or two devices—one interface is probably adequate.

A second interface allows this arrangement:

text


Built-in Wi‑Fi       → hotel Wi‑Fi/WAN
USB Wi‑Fi adapter    → your private Wi‑Fi/LAN
That improves things because:

Your private AP can use a different channel.
The two wireless links can operate more independently.
You can use 5 GHz for your devices if the USB adapter supports it.
Your private network is less affected by the hotel’s channel selection.
Simultaneous traffic is handled more smoothly.
It does not double your internet speed: the hotel connection remains the bottleneck, and the Zero 2 W’s processor, USB bus, hotel bandwidth, and radio interference still matter. But it can substantially improve consistency and local Wi-Fi performance.

There is an additional option:

text


Built-in Wi‑Fi       → hotel Wi‑Fi/WAN
USB Ethernet adapter → wired LAN or separate access point
That is often more stable than a second USB Wi‑Fi adapter, but you would need a separate Wi-Fi access point if your client devices need wireless access.

For your project, I’d choose:

Simplest: one built-in interface, acceptable for basic travel use.
Best compact wireless design: Zero 2 W plus a USB 5 GHz Wi-Fi adapter.
Most reliable: Zero 2 W using hotel Wi-Fi as WAN and Ethernet to a separate access point.
Best overall travel-router experience: a purpose-built dual-radio travel router rather than a Zero.
Use a powered USB OTG hub or a carefully selected low-power adapter; the Pi Zero’s USB power budget can be marginal with some Wi-Fi dongles. Also verify that the adapter has Linux drivers and supports access-point mode.

Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W Travel Router — Design Brief

Goal

Use the USB Wi-Fi adapter as the hotel/WAN connection, preferably over 5 GHz, and use the built-in Wi-Fi as your private local access point for all devices.

text


Hotel 5 GHz Wi‑Fi
        │
        │ USB Wi‑Fi adapter: wlan1
        ▼
Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W
  routing, DHCP, DNS, firewall, NAT
        ▲
        │ Built-in Wi‑Fi: wlan0
        │ private 2.4 GHz network
        ▼
Your devices
Here, “5G” means 5 GHz Wi-Fi, not cellular 5G.

Hardware

Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W
microSD card, preferably 16 GB or larger
USB OTG adapter or powered OTG hub
Linux-compatible USB Wi-Fi adapter with:
5 GHz client mode
Linux driver support
WPA2/WPA3 support as needed
USB-C or micro-USB power supply suitable for the Pi and adapter
Case with ventilation
Optional: physical reset button and status LED
The USB adapter will consume power from the Pi, and some adapters may require a powered hub. The Pi Zero 2 W’s USB connection is USB 2.0, so it will limit peak throughput even if the Wi-Fi adapter is much faster.

Interfaces

Assign the interfaces like this:

Interface	Role	Example
wlan1	USB adapter, hotel/WAN client	DHCP from hotel
wlan0	Built-in radio, private AP	192.168.50.1/24
The interface names may appear in the opposite order depending on the adapter and operating system. Confirm them with:

bash


ip link
Do not rely permanently on wlan0 and wlan1 until you verify which physical adapter each name represents.

Software stack

Use either:

OpenWrt, for a dedicated router-style system; or
Raspberry Pi OS Lite, using:
NetworkManager or wpa_supplicant for the hotel connection
hostapd for the local access point
dnsmasq or NetworkManager for DHCP/DNS
nftables for firewall and NAT
For this particular hardware, Raspberry Pi OS Lite may be easier if you are already comfortable with Linux. OpenWrt provides a cleaner router interface but may require more effort to get the USB adapter working.

Network design

WAN

The USB adapter connects to the hotel:

text


wlan1 → DHCP from hotel
The Pi receives an address automatically from the hotel network.

LAN

The built-in adapter provides your private network:

text


wlan0 → 192.168.50.1/24
Suggested DHCP range:

text


192.168.50.100–192.168.50.200
Use a subnet unlikely to conflict with the hotel:

text


192.168.50.0/24
The Pi should provide:

DHCP
DNS forwarding
IPv4 routing
NAT/masquerading
Firewalling between LAN and WAN
Access-point configuration

Configure wlan0 as a 2.4 GHz access point:

text


SSID:      YourTravelNetwork
Security:  WPA2-Personal/AES
Channel:   Auto or manually selected
Address:   192.168.50.1
All your devices connect only to YourTravelNetwork. They should not connect directly to the hotel network.

Use a fixed SSID and password if you want your devices to reconnect automatically on future trips.

Firewall behavior

The desired traffic flow is:

text


192.168.50.0/24 → wlan1 → hotel Internet
Enable IPv4 forwarding and NAT from the private LAN out through wlan1.

The firewall should:

Allow LAN-to-WAN traffic
Allow established and related return traffic
Block unsolicited WAN-to-LAN connections
Allow DHCP and DNS from local clients
Optionally block local clients from accessing the Pi’s administrative services except from one management device
Do not bridge wlan0 and wlan1; route between them using NAT.

Hotel login workflow

Boot the Pi.
Connect wlan1 to the hotel’s 5 GHz network.
Connect your phone or laptop to the Pi’s private SSID.
Open a plain HTTP page to trigger the hotel captive portal.
Complete the hotel login.
Use all other devices through the Pi’s private network.
Some hotels require a MAC-address registration or periodically expire the login. Plan for a way to access the Pi’s configuration page or SSH into it when reauthentication is needed.

Recommended management features

Add these after the basic router works:

Local administration page at http://192.168.50.1
A scan button for nearby hotel networks
A form to enter the hotel Wi-Fi password
A button to reconnect wlan1
Status display showing:
Hotel SSID
WAN IP address
Signal strength
Captive-portal state
Local client count
Automatic restart of the USB Wi-Fi connection if it drops
Read-only or protected configuration backup
A physical reset button that restores only the hotel/WAN settings, not your private SSID
Main risks

The USB Wi-Fi adapter may not have a usable Linux driver.
Some adapters support 5 GHz client mode but not all regulatory domains or channels.
The Pi’s USB power supply may be insufficient.
USB 2.0 limits maximum throughput.
The hotel may use captive portals, client isolation, or MAC registration.
The built-in local AP is 2.4 GHz only.
The recommended first milestone is:

Get wlan1 connected to ordinary home Wi-Fi.
Make wlan0 broadcast your private SSID.
Verify DHCP.
Add NAT and firewall rules.
Test the complete setup with the hotel captive portal.
